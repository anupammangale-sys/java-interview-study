Study material

Stop calling it

A service you depend on is failing. Everything in this episode is about one question: when do you stop calling it, and what do you do instead. The circuit breaker is built twice, once by hand in about ninety lines so the state machine is visible, and once with Resilience4j 2.2.0. They agree, until they do not, and the run where they disagreed changed what this page says.

Revision mode is on. Explanations are hidden. Try to answer each prompt out loud before switching back.

01 Four patterns, and why the order matters

  • Name the four patterns and say what each one protects.
  • In what order do you wrap them, and what goes wrong if you swap two?
  • Why is a retry dangerous on its own?
  • Which one do you add first if you can only have one?

The four. They get listed together because they are usually used together, and each one protects something different. Being able to say what each protects is most of the answer.

PatternWhat it doesWhat it protects
TimeoutGives up waiting after a set time Your threads. Without one, a hung call holds a thread for ever and your service dies of something that happened somewhere else.
RetryTries again after a failure The caller, from a blip that would have worked a moment later. It protects nothing else, and left alone it makes everything worse.
Circuit breakerStops calling once enough calls have failed The service you are calling, and your own latency. It is the only one that reduces load on something that is already struggling.
BulkheadLimits how many calls run at once Everything else you do. It stops one sick dependency taking every thread and dragging down work that has nothing to do with it.

The order is not a style choice, and it is measurable. Resilience4j documents the order as Retry, then CircuitBreaker, then RateLimiter, then TimeLimiter, then Bulkhead, outermost first. Here is what happens when the first two are swapped.

Twenty logical calls, three attempts each, against a service that is down. Both breakers open after the same number of failures:

the same test in both projectsreal output
                                       calls that   breaker
                                       reached it     state
  retry OUTSIDE, breaker sees attempts          5      OPEN
  retry INSIDE, breaker sees one call          15      OPEN

Fifteen calls instead of five, to a service that is already down.

The reason is simple once you see it. With the retry on the outside, every attempt passes through the breaker, so the breaker counts three failures per logical call and opens when it should. With the retry on the inside, the breaker sees one call that secretly made three, so it counts one failure for every three real ones and takes three times as long to notice.

That is also the answer to "why is a retry dangerous on its own". A retry multiplies load at exactly the moment the thing you are calling is least able to take it. Three attempts from every caller during an incident is a threefold traffic increase aimed at something already on the floor.

What each pattern is forclick one

Pick a pattern.

If you can only have one, have a timeout. This is worth saying because it is the least glamorous answer and the correct one. A circuit breaker cannot help if every call hangs for ever, because your threads are gone before the breaker has counted anything. Everything else in this episode assumes calls eventually return.

Common wrong answers

"Add retries to make it more reliable." On its own a retry makes an incident worse. It needs a limit, a delay, jitter, and something above it that can decide to stop entirely.

"The circuit breaker protects my service." Partly. Its main job is to stop hammering the service that is failing. What protects your service from a sick dependency is the bulkhead and the timeout.

"The order does not really matter." Measured above: 5 calls against 15, from swapping two decorators.

Follow-up questions

Why is a timeout the first thing to add?

Because without one, a slow dependency does not slow you down, it stops you completely, and none of the other patterns can save you.

Think about where the thread goes. A web request holds one thread for its whole life, including all the time it spends waiting. If a downstream call normally takes 50 milliseconds and starts taking 30 seconds, each thread does six hundred times less work. A pool of 200 threads that comfortably served your traffic now serves almost none of it, and requests to completely unrelated endpoints start queueing and failing. Nothing in your code changed and nothing in your code is wrong.

A circuit breaker cannot rescue that on its own, because the breaker only counts calls that have finished. If calls never finish, there are no failures to count, and the breaker sits closed while every thread is stuck inside it. That is the specific reason the timeout has to exist first.

The number to have ready: pick the timeout from the downstream service's real latency, not from a round number. Something like the 99th percentile plus a margin. A timeout of 30 seconds on a call that normally takes 50 milliseconds is the same as having no timeout at all, because the damage is done long before it fires.

Which failures should be retried, and which should never be?

The test is the same one from a well designed exception hierarchy: could trying again possibly work?

Retry these. A connection timeout, a connection refused, an HTTP 503 or 429, a deadlock the database rolled back. Something was temporarily unavailable and the same request might succeed in a moment.

Never retry these. A 400, a 401, a 403, a 404, a validation failure, anything where the request itself is the problem. The response will be identical every time, so a retry is pure waste and it triples the load for nothing.

The dangerous middle. A 500 or a read timeout, on a call that changes something. You do not know whether the work happened. Retrying might charge a card twice; not retrying might lose the order. Neither is safe, which is why the real answer is an idempotency key on the request so that a duplicate is harmless, and only then a retry.

Say this out loud in an interview: a retry is only safe on an operation that is safe to repeat. Reads almost always are. Writes are only if you made them so.

Where should these live, in the application or in the infrastructure?

Both, and they do different jobs, which is why the question comes up.

A service mesh or a sidecar can do timeouts, retries, circuit breaking and rate limiting for every service at once, with no code and no library. That is a real advantage: it applies uniformly, it is configured by the platform team, and a service written in another language gets it for free.

What it cannot do is know what your call means. The mesh sees an HTTP request. It does not know that this one is a payment that must never be retried blindly, or that this other one has a perfectly good cached answer to fall back on, or that a failure here should degrade the page rather than fail it. Fallbacks in particular are application knowledge and cannot be pushed down.

The shape that works is the mesh handling the broad defaults, connection timeouts, retries on safe methods, a general breaker, and the application handling the decisions that need to know what the call is for. The trap to name is doing both without noticing: three retries in the mesh and three in the application is nine attempts, and neither team believes they configured that.

How do these patterns interact with a load balancer having several instances?

It changes what a failure means, and it is worth thinking about before you tune anything.

If the downstream service has ten instances and one is broken, roughly one call in ten fails. Your breaker sees a 10 percent failure rate and, quite correctly, does not open, because ninety percent of the service is fine and cutting it off entirely would turn a small problem into a total outage.

That is usually the right behaviour, and it is why per instance circuit breaking exists in service meshes: they can eject the one bad instance from the pool and leave the rest alone. From inside your application you normally cannot see which instance answered, so you cannot make that distinction.

The practical consequence for your own configuration: a breaker in the application is protecting you from the service as a whole being unwell, not from one bad instance. Tune it for that. If a single instance failing is a common problem in your setup, the fix belongs at the load balancer or the mesh, with health checks and ejection, not in a threshold you keep lowering until it catches it.

02 Writing a circuit breaker

  • Describe the three states and what moves it between them.
  • What is half open for, and why not just close it after the wait?
  • Write the state machine on a whiteboard.
  • What should a call return when the breaker is open?

It is three states and two counters. Written out in full it is about ninety lines including comments, which is worth knowing because it stops the pattern feeling like something only a library can do.

The state machineclick a state

Pick a state.

The core of it, as actually written:

handbuilt/Breaker.javathe decision
// Ask before calling. False means refuse without touching the service.
public synchronized boolean allow() {
    if (state == State.OPEN) {
        if (System.currentTimeMillis() - openedAt >= openMillis) {
            moveTo(State.HALF_OPEN);        // time to try again
            trialsStarted = 0; trialsSucceeded = 0;
        } else {
            refused++; return false;      // still cooling off
        }
    }
    if (state == State.HALF_OPEN) {
        if (trialsStarted >= trialCalls) { refused++; return false; }
        trialsStarted++;
    }
    allowed++;
    return true;
}

What it bought, measured. One hundred calls to a service that is down, where each failing call takes 20 milliseconds to fail:

Calls that reached itRefused without calling Total time
no protection10002066 ms
hand written breaker595103 ms
Resilience4j595125 ms

Both versions agree, which is the reason for writing it twice. The library is not doing anything mysterious: it is this state machine with more careful bookkeeping.

Note the second column. Ninety five calls were answered without touching the network at all. That is where the twenty times speed up comes from, and it is also the thing people forget: an open breaker makes your service faster, not slower, because failing immediately beats waiting to fail.

Common wrong answers

"It stops calls when the service is down." True and it skips the part that gets asked: how does it decide, and how does it ever start again. Half open is the interesting state.

"After the wait it closes again." No. It goes to half open and lets a small number of trial calls through. Closing blindly would send the full traffic straight back at a service that may still be broken.

"The breaker retries for you." It does not retry anything. It decides whether a call is allowed. Retrying is a separate pattern that sits outside it.

Follow-up questions

What exactly is half open for?

It is the state that lets the breaker find out whether the service has recovered without betting everything on the answer.

Think about what the alternatives cost. If the breaker closed automatically after the wait, the full traffic would go straight back at a service that may still be broken, and you would have engineered a cycle: recover slightly, get flattened, open again. If it never reopened on its own, somebody would have to notice and restart something, which is not a design.

Half open threads that needle. A small fixed number of calls is allowed through, two in the demo here. Everything else is still refused. If those trial calls succeed, the breaker closes and normal traffic resumes. If any of them fails, it opens again immediately and the clock restarts, so a service that is still down absorbs two calls rather than everything you have.

Two details worth having. The trial calls are real customer requests, not synthetic ones, so a few real people pay the cost of finding out. And the number of trials is a genuine trade: more trials means a more reliable verdict and more load on something that might still be sick.

What should the call return when the breaker is open?

Something immediate, and the choice is yours rather than the breaker's. In Resilience4j an open breaker throws CallNotPermittedException, and what you do with it is the interesting decision.

A fallback when you have something honest to say: a cached price with a note that it may be out of date, an empty list of recommendations, a default shipping estimate. This is usually the right answer and it is chapter five.

A clear failure when there is no honest fallback. A 503 with a Retry-After header tells the caller both that this is temporary and roughly when to come back. Much better than a 500, which tells them nothing.

Never a silent success. Returning an empty result that looks like a real answer is the worst option, because the caller cannot tell the difference between "there is nothing" and "we could not find out". That distinction matters and once you have thrown it away nobody downstream can get it back.

The thing that makes this answer good rather than adequate: whatever you return should make it obvious in your metrics that the breaker was open, so a degraded service does not look like a healthy one with fewer results.

Should there be one breaker per service, or per endpoint?

Per thing that can fail independently, which in practice usually means per endpoint or per small group of endpoints, not one for the whole downstream service.

The reason is that services rarely fail as a unit. A payments service might have a search endpoint hitting a struggling database while the charge endpoint is perfectly fine. One breaker for the whole service would cut off charging because search is slow, which turns a partial problem into a total one.

The cost of going too far the other way is real too. A breaker per endpoint means each one sees a fraction of the traffic, and a breaker with very few calls cannot measure a failure rate meaningfully. That is what minimumNumberOfCalls is for, and on a quiet endpoint the breaker may effectively never have enough data to act.

The rule that survives contact with reality: group by the resource behind the call, not by the service in front of it. If three endpoints all hit the same database table, they fail together and they can share a breaker. If one hits a cache and another hits a third party, they are different failures and deserve different breakers.

Is a hand written breaker ever the right choice?

Rarely, and the honest reason is in chapter three rather than in line count.

The ninety line version in this episode matched the library exactly for a service that was completely down: five calls, ninety five refused. If that were the whole problem, the library would be hard to justify.

It fell apart on a service that was failing only some of the time, which is the far more common real situation. Counting failures in a row missed the problem entirely in four out of ten identical runs. Fixing that means a sliding window, which means deciding between counting calls and counting time, which means metrics to tune it with, and by then you have written the library badly instead of using it.

So the case for the library is not that it is shorter. It is that somebody has already thought about sliding windows, slow call rates, half open bookkeeping and the metrics you need to see what it is doing. Writing one yourself is a good way to understand it and a poor way to ship it.

The exception worth naming: a small internal tool with one dependency and no operations team may genuinely be better off with twenty lines it fully understands than with a dependency and a configuration nobody will ever tune.

03 What the configuration decides

  • A service fails 30 percent of the time. Should the breaker open?
  • What does the sliding window size actually change?
  • Count based or time based window, and when does it matter?
  • What is a slow call rate and why does it exist?

This is the chapter that changed while it was being written. The plan was to show that a hand written breaker is not as good as the library. The measurement said something more interesting.

A service that is not down, just unwell: it fails 30 percent of the time. Three breakers, ten runs each, 300 calls per run. The number recorded is how many calls reached the service before the breaker opened.

ten runs of each, same seedsreal output
hand written, 5 failures IN A ROW
  per run: 236 188 never 234 never 204 7 never 51 never
  opened in 6 of 10 runs, from 7 to 236 calls, average 153

  opened in 10 of 10 runs, from 5 to 95 calls, average 31

The second and third rows use the same 50 percent threshold against the same service. One opened on every single run. The other never opened at all. The only difference is the size of the window they measure over.

That is worth sitting with, because it is the opposite of how the configuration reads. The threshold looks like the important number. It is not. The window size decides whether the threshold is measuring the service or measuring noise.

Why the narrow window opens. With only 10 samples of a process that fails 30 percent of the time, four or more failures in a window is common by chance. So a window of 10 crosses 50 percent regularly even though the true rate never does. The wide window averages that out and reports something close to the real 30 percent, which is below the threshold, so it correctly stays closed.

Neither is simply right, and that is the answer to give:

WindowReactsRiskSuits
Narrow, tens of callsFast, within seconds Opens on a run of bad luck when the service is fine High traffic endpoints where a real problem shows up immediately and a brief false open costs little
Wide, hundreds of callsSlow Absorbs a lot of failures before acting Anywhere a false open is expensive, and low traffic endpoints where a small window is mostly noise

And the first row is the argument for the library. Counting failures in a row missed the problem entirely in four of ten identical runs, and when it did notice, it took anywhere from 7 to 236 calls. One success resets the count, so a service failing 30 percent of the time keeps it closed almost indefinitely. That is not a tuning problem, it is the wrong measurement.

What this page said before the demo was run

An earlier version claimed a consecutive failure breaker "barely notices" a partly failing service. The first measurement disagreed: it opened, after 14 calls. The claim was wrong and was replaced by the ten run comparison above, which says something more useful. The interesting property is not whether it notices but how much the answer moves between identical runs.

Common wrong answers

"Set the failure threshold to 50 percent and you are done." Both Resilience4j rows above are set to exactly that. They behave completely differently.

"A smaller window is more responsive, so it is better." It is more responsive to noise as well as to problems. On a healthy service it will open on bad luck.

"Copy the defaults." The defaults are a starting point chosen without knowing your traffic. On a quiet endpoint they may never gather enough calls to act at all.

Follow-up questions

Count based or time based window? How do you choose?

By how steady your traffic is, and the failure mode of each choice is the thing to name.

A count based window holds the last N calls. It is predictable in what it measures, and its weakness is quiet endpoints: if an endpoint gets ten calls an hour, a window of 100 holds ten hours of history, so the breaker is reacting to this morning when it is the afternoon. A problem that started five minutes ago is invisible.

A time based window holds the calls from the last N seconds. It always reflects now, which is what you usually want, and its weakness is the opposite: during a quiet minute the window might hold three calls, and two failures out of three is a 66 percent failure rate that opens the breaker on almost no evidence.

Both have minimumNumberOfCalls, and that setting matters far more on a time based window because it is the only thing standing between you and acting on three data points.

The practical default: time based for anything user facing with steady traffic, because "the last ten seconds" is what an incident actually means. Count based when traffic is bursty or low, so the window is guaranteed to contain enough calls to mean something.

What is the slow call rate, and why is it separate from the failure rate?

Because the worst kind of failure is not an error, it is an answer that arrives too late to be useful, and a failure rate cannot see it at all.

Resilience4j lets you set a duration above which a call counts as slow, and a percentage of slow calls that will open the breaker. A service returning perfect responses in eight seconds has a zero percent failure rate and is destroying you, because every one of those calls is holding a thread of yours for eight seconds. The breaker needs a way to treat that as a failure, and the slow call rate is it.

This is the setting most people have never heard of and it is often more useful than the failure rate, because services degrade before they break. Latency climbing is the early warning; errors are what happens after.

It also interacts with the timeout in a way worth explaining. If your timeout is two seconds, every slow call already becomes a failure at two seconds, so the slow call threshold only matters below that. Setting it to something like one second gives you a breaker that opens while calls are still technically succeeding, which is the whole point.

How would you tune these settings for a real service?

From measurements, in an order, and the honest first step is that you cannot do it from first principles.

Start with the timeout, because everything else depends on calls finishing. Take the downstream service's real latency distribution and set the timeout somewhere above the 99th percentile with a margin. If you do not have that number, getting it is the task before this one.

Then the window, from traffic volume. It needs to contain enough calls that a rate means something, which is a few tens at least, and few enough that it reflects now rather than an hour ago. Calls per second times the number of seconds you want to react within is a reasonable starting calculation.

Then the threshold, from what is normal. If the service normally fails 2 percent of the time, a 50 percent threshold is enormous headroom and it will let a bad situation run for a long time. If it normally fails 20 percent, a 25 percent threshold will open constantly. The threshold is relative to the baseline, and you need the baseline first.

Then watch it. Resilience4j publishes the state, the failure rate and the number of buffered calls as metrics. A breaker you cannot see the state of is a breaker you cannot tune, and the most common real problem is one that has been sitting open for two weeks with nobody aware.

What happens when the breaker opens in production and nobody expected it?

Usually a confusing incident, because the symptom does not look like the cause, and that is worth being ready for.

What people see is your service returning errors or degraded results very fast. Latency graphs look great, because refused calls return in microseconds. Error rate is up but the downstream service you are blaming reports almost no traffic and looks perfectly healthy, because your breaker is not calling it. Two teams then spend twenty minutes disagreeing about whose problem it is.

Everything that avoids that is preparation rather than cleverness. Emit the breaker state as a metric and put it on the dashboard next to the error rate. Log the transition, once, with the failure rate that caused it, so there is a timestamp to correlate against. Make the response when open distinguishable in your metrics from a normal failure.

The other half is knowing whether it was right. A breaker that opened because the downstream really was failing did its job. A breaker that opened because the window was too narrow and it caught a run of bad luck is a false alarm, and the measurement in this chapter is what that looks like. You can only tell the two apart if you recorded the failure rate at the moment it tripped.

04 Timeouts and retries

  • A call hangs for three seconds. What does a timeout actually free?
  • Why is exponential backoff not enough on its own?
  • How many retries, and how do you choose?
  • What is the difference between a connect timeout and a read timeout?

What a timeout does, and what it does not. A call that takes three seconds, with and without a 200 millisecond limit:

handbuilt/Isolation.javareal output
no timeout      waited 3007 ms, got "eventually"
200 ms timeout  waited 213 ms, got "gave up"

The timeout freed the caller after 213 milliseconds. Whether the work itself stopped is a completely separate question, and this is the part that gets missed.

Cancelling a task only interrupts the thread running it. Interruption is a request, not a command. Code sitting in Thread.sleep or a blocking queue will notice. Code in a tight loop, or inside a socket read that does not have its own timeout, will not, and it carries on holding that thread until it finishes on its own.

So a timeout without a way to stop the work still leaks threads. The caller is free and the worker is not. This is why HTTP clients have their own connect and read timeouts: those stop the socket operation itself, which an interrupt cannot do.

TimeoutCoversSensible size
ConnectEstablishing the connection only Short, one or two seconds. If it takes longer than that, the host is unreachable rather than busy
Read or responseWaiting for the answer once connected From the downstream service's real latency, above its 99th percentile
Overall or callThe whole operation including any retries The one that protects your caller. It has to account for the retries, which people forget

The arithmetic people get wrong: a two second read timeout with three attempts and backoff is not a two second operation. It is up to two seconds, then a wait, then two more, then a longer wait, then two more. Six seconds of calls plus the waits. If the caller upstream gives up after three seconds, your retries are finishing into a void.

Backoff and jitter. Waiting longer between attempts stops one client hammering a struggling service. It does nothing about the case that actually causes outages, which is every client failing at the same moment and therefore retrying at the same moment.

Plain backoff moves the whole crowd together. Adding jitter, which means waiting a random amount up to the backoff rather than exactly the backoff, spreads them out. It is one multiplication and it is the difference between a provider getting one wave of traffic and getting the same wave repeatedly.

Common wrong answers

"The timeout kills the call." It stops your thread waiting. The work may well continue. Measured above: the caller was free in 213 ms and the task had three seconds still to run.

"Exponential backoff solves the retry storm." It solves one client hammering. It does nothing about many clients that failed together, because it moves them all by the same amount.

"Retry until it works." Without a limit, a permanently broken call retries for ever and holds a slot the whole time.

Follow-up questions

How many retries, and how do you pick the number?

Work backwards from how long the whole operation is allowed to take, because that is the number the business actually cares about and the retry count follows from it.

With a base wait of 100 milliseconds and doubling, the waits are 100, 200, 400, 800. Three retries is about 1.5 seconds of waiting on top of the calls themselves. Ten retries is nearly two minutes, and the last few waits are longer than any user will stay. So the question "how many retries" is really "how long may this take", and two or three is common because that keeps the total in seconds.

Then let the operation decide. Something a person is waiting for gets one or two attempts and then an honest failure, because a slow answer is worse than a fast no. Something in a background job can afford far more, because nobody is watching and the alternative is losing the work.

Two things worth adding. Set a total time limit as well as an attempt count, so a large base wait cannot quietly turn three attempts into a minute. And remember that retries multiply across layers: three in the client and three in a gateway is nine attempts, and each team believes they configured three.

What is the difference between a connect timeout and a read timeout in practice?

They fail for different reasons, and telling them apart tells you what is wrong.

A connect timeout fires when you cannot establish a connection at all. In practice that means the host is unreachable, DNS resolved to something wrong, a firewall is dropping packets silently, or the service is not listening. None of those get better in the next few seconds, which is why the timeout should be short. A connect timeout of 30 seconds is 30 seconds of a thread spent learning something you could have known in one.

A read timeout fires when the connection was established and the answer did not arrive. That means the service is alive and slow: overloaded, stuck on a lock, waiting on its own downstream. It might genuinely come back, which is why this is the one to size from real latency rather than from a round number.

The diagnostic value is the part worth saying. A spike in connect timeouts points at the network or at instances being gone. A spike in read timeouts points at the service being alive and struggling. They call for different responses, and a single "timeout" number in a dashboard hides which one you have.

One trap: connect timeouts can be misleading when connection pooling is in use, because a healthy pool reuses connections and rarely connects at all. A sudden burst of connect timeouts can mean the pool was exhausted or reset, not that the network changed.

Is it ever right to retry immediately, with no wait at all?

Occasionally yes, and knowing when shows you have thought about what the failure means rather than applying a rule.

An immediate retry makes sense when the failure is almost certainly about the connection rather than the service. A pooled connection that was closed by the other end while it sat idle fails instantly on first use, and the second attempt gets a fresh connection and works. Waiting achieves nothing because nothing needs time to recover. Many HTTP clients and database drivers do exactly this internally, which is worth knowing because it means you may already have one retry you did not configure.

The same is true of an optimistic locking failure. Two transactions collided, one lost, and the right response is to read the current state and try again now. The conflict is already over.

Where an immediate retry is wrong is anything that suggests the service is under strain: a 503, a 429, a read timeout. There the failure is telling you it needs time, and retrying instantly is the behaviour that turns a struggling service into a dead one.

A reasonable pattern is one immediate retry for connection level failures, then backoff with jitter for everything else. Worth saying that the first retry is nearly free and the later ones need care.

What is the interaction between a timeout and a circuit breaker?

They need each other, and the direction of the dependency is the interesting part.

The breaker needs the timeout to work at all. A breaker counts calls that have finished. If calls hang for ever, nothing finishes, so the breaker never records a failure and sits closed while every thread is stuck inside it. Without a timeout, the breaker is decoration.

The timeout needs the breaker to stop being expensive. A two second timeout on a service that is down means every single call costs you two seconds of a thread before failing. At a hundred calls a second that is two hundred threads permanently occupied doing nothing. The breaker turns those two seconds into microseconds once it has seen enough failures, which is exactly the hundredfold improvement measured in chapter two.

The sizing detail that connects them: a timeout should count as a failure to the breaker, and in Resilience4j whether it does depends on which exception the timeout produces and whether you have told the breaker to record it. Getting that wrong gives you a breaker that never opens during precisely the incident it was built for, and it is a genuinely common misconfiguration because everything looks correct.

05 Designing a fallback

  • The breaker is open. What do you return?
  • When is a fallback worse than an error?
  • What makes a fallback safe?
  • How do you stop a fallback hiding an outage?

This is the chapter with no measurement, and that is worth saying. Everything before it is arithmetic. What to return when a dependency is gone is a judgement about your product, and an interviewer asking it is testing whether you think past the code.

The question that decides it: can the user still get what they came for without this? Not "can I return something", but "is what I return still true".

FallbackGood forThe risk
A cached answer Data that changes slowly. A product description, a category list, a currency rate. Being wrong for as long as the outage lasts. Fine for a description, not for stock levels during a sale.
A safe default Something with an obvious conservative value. No recommendations, standard shipping, no discount applied. The default has to be safe in the direction that matters. Defaulting a fraud check to "passed" is how you lose money.
Degraded but honest Showing the page without the part that failed, and saying so. Needs the front end to cope with a missing section, which is a design decision made long before the outage.
Queue it and answer later Writes that do not need to complete now. Sending an email, updating a recommendation profile. Only works if the caller genuinely does not need the result, and you now own a queue that has to be drained.
Fail clearly Anything where a wrong answer is worse than no answer. Payments, balances, permissions. None, and it is underused. A fast honest failure is a legitimate design.
The dependency is down. What do you return?pick a scenario

Pick a scenario.

The rule that covers most of it. A fallback is safe when the caller can tell the difference between a real answer and a fallback, or when they genuinely do not need to. It is dangerous the moment a made up answer is indistinguishable from a true one.

Two fallbacks that look reasonable and are not.

Returning an empty list when a search service is down. The page says "no results", the user believes there is nothing, and they leave. Nothing errored, no alert fired, and the business lost a customer to something that looked like a working page. An error would have been better, because it is true.

Defaulting a permission check to "allowed" so the site stays up. It will stay up right up until somebody notices, and the failure mode is a security incident rather than an outage. The direction a default fails in matters more than whether it exists.

The one that hides an outage

A cached fallback that works well is the most dangerous kind, because the service looks healthy. Latency is fine, errors are zero, and a dependency has been down for three days. The fix is not to avoid the fallback, it is to make it visible: count fallback responses as their own metric and alert on the rate, not on errors.

Common wrong answers

"Return an empty list, it is safer than an error." It is quieter, not safer. The caller cannot tell "there is nothing" from "we could not find out", and that distinction is often the whole point.

"Always have a fallback." For a payment, a balance or a permission check there is no honest fallback, and inventing one turns an outage into a correctness problem.

"Fall back to a second provider." Reasonable and rarely as simple as it sounds: the second provider is usually less tested, may have different semantics, and gets its first real traffic during your worst hour.

Follow-up questions

How do you stop a fallback hiding an outage for days?

By measuring the fallback rather than the failure, because the whole point of a good fallback is that the failure stops being visible.

The mechanism is straightforward and rarely done. Count every fallback response as its own metric, separate from errors and separate from successes. Then alert on that count being above zero for longer than some period, rather than on the error rate, which will be flat.

The reason it gets missed is that dashboards are usually built around the golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation. A dependency behind a working fallback moves none of them. Latency improves, because a cached answer is faster than a real one. Errors go to zero. Everything looks better than usual, which is the tell.

Two extra habits worth naming. Put the age of the cached data in the response or at least in the logs, so "we are serving a fallback" and "we are serving a fallback from Tuesday" are different alerts. And exercise the fallback deliberately, because a fallback path that has never run in production is code you are hoping works during the one hour it matters.

Where should the fallback live in the code?

Close to the caller who knows what the data is for, not next to the client that fetches it, and the reason is that the right fallback depends on the use.

The same call to a pricing service has different correct answers depending on who asked. On a product listing page, a slightly stale cached price is fine and much better than a gap. On the checkout page, charging a stale price is a real problem and the honest response is to fail and ask them to try again. A fallback hidden inside the pricing client cannot tell those apart, so it will be wrong for one of them.

The shape that works: the client throws, and each caller decides. Resilience4j supports this directly, since the decorated supplier throws CallNotPermittedException and the caller catches it, and Spring's @CircuitBreaker takes a fallbackMethod per method rather than per client.

The exception worth naming: a genuinely universal fallback, such as an empty list of optional recommendations that nothing depends on, can live in the client. But if you find yourself writing "unless the caller is checkout" inside a client, the fallback is in the wrong place.

What do you tell the user when there is no good fallback?

The truth, at the right level of detail, and this gets asked more than people expect because it is where engineering meets the product.

The three things a user needs: that it is not their fault, that it is temporary, and what to do now. "Something went wrong" fails all three. "We cannot reach our payment provider right now, your basket is saved, please try again in a few minutes" satisfies all three and takes no more code.

Preserving their work is the part engineers most often skip. An outage that loses a half filled form turns a two minute annoyance into a lost customer. If the failure happens on submit, the state should still be there on retry, and that is a design decision made long before the incident.

For an API rather than a person, the equivalents are concrete: return 503 rather than 500, so the caller knows it is worth retrying, and include a Retry-After header so they know roughly when. If you have a request id, return it, because it is the difference between a support conversation that takes five minutes and one that takes a day.

Should a fallback ever call a different service?

Sometimes, and it is worth being sceptical about, because it is the fallback that most often fails when it is finally needed.

The appeal is obvious: a second payment provider, a second maps API, a second email service. If the first is down, use the other one. For genuinely commodity services with the same shape, this can work well.

Three reasons it disappoints. The second provider has almost no production traffic, so its integration is the least tested code you own, and it gets its first real load during your worst hour. The semantics are rarely identical: different error codes, different rounding, different limits, different idempotency behaviour. And you now maintain two integrations for ever, including the one nobody uses, which quietly rots.

The thing that makes it work is exercising it. Send a small percentage of normal traffic to the secondary all the time, so it is warm, monitored and known to work. A failover path that only runs during failures is a plan, not a capability.

And for anything that moves money, the harder question is not whether you can call the other provider but whether you can tell afterwards which one took the payment. That is a reconciliation problem and it usually dwarfs the routing.

The failure this prevents. A pool of 10 threads serves two dependencies. The reports service has gone slow, two seconds a call. The prices service is completely healthy at 10 milliseconds. Twenty report requests arrive, then twenty price requests.

The measurement is the slowest price call, timed from submission, because the entire cost of a starved pool is the wait before the work even starts:

That is the failure worth being able to describe: one sick dependency makes everything slow, including things that do not use it, because they share a pool. The bulkhead fixes it by refusing 16 report calls, which is the trade. You deliberately give up some of the sick dependency to keep everything else moving.

A measurement that nearly went out wrong

The first version of this demo timed the price calls from inside the task and reported 25 ms against 15 ms, which proved nothing at all. The queue wait was the entire effect and it was being measured out of the result. Timing from submission is what turned a meaningless table into a sixty six times difference.

A thousand instant calls a second pass a bulkhead of 4 happily, because none of them overlap for long, and are cut down hard by a rate limiter of 10 per second. Four calls that each take a minute pass a rate limiter of 10 per second happily and fill a bulkhead of 4 completely. A service under strain often needs both.

Common wrong answers

"A bulkhead is just a thread pool." A semaphore bulkhead uses the caller's thread and only counts permits. There is a thread pool variant, and they behave very differently under load.

"A bulkhead and a rate limiter do the same thing." One is about concurrency, the other about frequency. Measured above: same code, completely different numbers.

"Make the pool bigger." That buys time and does not fix anything. The sick dependency will fill whatever you give it, and now you have more threads doing nothing.

Follow-up questions

Semaphore or thread pool bulkhead? What actually differs?

Where the work runs, and it changes what you can do about a slow call.

A semaphore bulkhead is a permit count. The call runs on the caller's own thread; the bulkhead only decides whether it may start. It is nearly free, it adds no context switching, and anything relying on the current thread, such as a request scoped bean or a security context, keeps working. Its limit is that it cannot impose a timeout, because there is nothing to interrupt except the caller itself.

A thread pool bulkhead runs the call on its own separate pool and hands the caller a future. That means you can time out and walk away, leaving the stuck call on the isolated pool where it cannot take your request threads. It also means the work runs on a different thread, so thread local state does not travel with it unless you carry it deliberately.

The practical guidance: semaphore by default, because it is simpler and cheaper. Thread pool when the dependency can hang in a way you cannot otherwise interrupt, which in practice means a client library with no timeout of its own.

Worth noting that this is what Hystrix did years ago, and Resilience4j's default is the semaphore because the thread pool version costs more than most calls need.

How do you size a bulkhead?

From how much of your capacity you are willing to lose to this one dependency, which is a different question from how much traffic it gets.

The starting sum is Little's law. Calls per second multiplied by the average duration in seconds gives the average number in flight. Twenty calls a second at 100 milliseconds each is two in flight on average, so a bulkhead of two is already tight and something like five gives headroom for bursts.

Then apply the real constraint. If the pool has 200 threads and this dependency can go slow, a bulkhead of 20 means the worst case is 20 threads stuck, leaving 180 for everything else. That is the number you actually care about, and it is why the answer is usually a small fraction of the pool rather than a comfortable multiple of normal traffic.

Two failure modes to name. Too large is the common one and it is invisible in normal operation: everything works fine until the dependency slows down, and then the bulkhead was never a limit at all. Too small shows up immediately as rejections during ordinary bursts, which is annoying but at least it is obvious.

And say the last part out loud: you size it, then you watch the rejection count. A bulkhead that has never rejected anything is either well sized or pointless, and only the metric can tell you which.

Should a rate limiter make callers wait, or reject them?

It depends entirely on who is waiting, and Resilience4j makes this a single setting, timeoutDuration, which is zero in the demo above.

Reject immediately when a person is waiting. A user's request holding a thread for two seconds waiting for a permit is a thread you cannot use and a user who thinks the site is broken. Fail fast, return 429, let them retry.

Wait for a permit when the caller is a background job with nowhere else to be. A batch that has ten thousand records to push through a provider limited to 100 a second should absolutely wait; rejecting would just mean writing your own retry loop around it, which is the same thing with more code.

The trap in the waiting version is that it turns a rate limit into a queue, and queues need a bound. If work arrives faster than the limit allows, and everyone waits, the number of waiting threads grows without limit and you have moved the failure rather than fixed it. That is why the timeout exists at all: it is the bound.

The answer that shows you have used one: set it to zero for anything user facing, set it to something small for internal calls, and never leave it unbounded.

Do these still matter with virtual threads?

The bulkhead's original reason weakens considerably. The rest matter as much as ever, and this is a good question to have a real position on because it is current.

The classic argument for a bulkhead is that platform threads are scarce, about a megabyte of stack each, so a few hundred is the practical limit and a slow dependency eating them is fatal. Virtual threads are cheap enough that a million is reasonable, and blocking one does not hold a platform thread. So the specific failure measured in this chapter, healthy calls queued behind a slow dependency because the pool was full, largely goes away.

What does not go away is everything downstream of your process. Each in flight call still holds a database connection, or a slot in an HTTP client's connection pool, or memory for its request and response. Those are still finite and still worth protecting, so the bulkhead's job shifts from protecting threads to protecting connections. That is a real shift, not a removal.

And the pressure moves outward. Ten thousand virtual threads all calling a struggling service is a much bigger hammer than two hundred platform threads were. If anything, the circuit breaker and the rate limiter become more important once the thread pool is no longer accidentally doing that job for you.

The line to finish on: virtual threads remove a limit, they do not remove the need for limits. They just mean you now have to choose them deliberately instead of inheriting them from the size of a pool.
